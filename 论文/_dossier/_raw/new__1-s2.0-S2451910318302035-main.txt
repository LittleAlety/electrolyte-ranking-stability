Review Article
Challenges with prediction of battery electrolyte
electrochemical stability window and guiding the
electrode – electrolyte stabilization
Oleg Borodin
Abstact
Progress in development of novel battery chemistries critically
depends on the stabilization of electrode – electrolyte in-
terfaces. Battery electrolytes are commonly selected to either
be electrochemically stable at the electrode, current collector,
and conductive additive surfaces or to decompose at these
interfaces electrochemically and form a kinetically protective
ionically-conducting but electronically-insulating passivation
film. Increasing energy density of electrical double layer (EDL)
capacitors, or supercapacitors, also requires electrolytes with
an extended electrochemical stability window. Quantum
chemistry (QC) calculations of the representative electrolyte
clusters or electrolyte components reacting with the electrode
surfaces provide molecular scale understanding of the key
electrolyte decomposition pathways under reductive or oxida-
tive conditions. Moreover, QC calculations show that the
electrolyte reduction and oxidation stability depends on the
solvent and salt partitioning within EDL near electrolytes and
the number of lithium cations complexing anions or solvents.
Thus, an electrolyte electrochemical stability could be
controlled by adjusting salt concentration, salt aggregation and
preferential solvent or anion partitioning within EDL, opening
new opportunities for enabling aggressive battery chemistries
and EDL capacitors with improved energy densities.

Introduction
Energy storage devices from lithium batteries to hybrid
and electrical double layer (EDL) capacitors play
increasingly important role in today’s life, with applica-
tions from electric mobility to portable electronics,
micro-grids, and uninterruptible sources of power for
critical infrastructure and services. Energy density of
lithium batteries is proportional to its operating voltage,
while energy density of EDL capacitors is proportional
to the square of an operating voltage. Development of
electrolytes with an increased electrochemical stability
window is one of the ways of making energy storage
devices lighter and more compact. Because electrolytes
are multifunctional materials, in addition to their elec-
trochemical stability at the electrode, current collector
and conductive additives, they also need to be elec-
tronically insulating, non-volatile, non-ﬂammable, ioni-
cally conducting, able to wet a separator, be
environmentally benign, low-cost and being able to
accommodate electrode volume change during battery
cycling and to prevent dissolution of electroactive
electrode materials. It is quite challenging to simulta-
neously satisfy all these criteria.
Recent progress in battery development increasingly
relies on the solvent, salt and additive combinations that
undergo redox reactions at electrode surfaces and form a
stable passivation layer that is ionically conducting but
electronically insulating, thus preventing further elec-
trolyte decomposition while still allowing volume
changes of the active material particles during metal
intercalation, conversion, or alloying reactions. The
passivation layer formed on the anode is called the solid
electrolyte interphase (SEI) to indicate that it could be
viewed as a new phase with its own properties such as
conductivity , transference number and mechanical
properties. It usually has thickness from 3 nm to 10 nm
[1,2]. The passivation layer on cathode is denoted as the
cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI) to highlight that it
is chemically different from the SEI formed on the
anode despite that it is formed as a result of electro-
chemical decomposition of the same electrolyte.
Obtaining fundamental understanding of the battery
electrolyte oxidation and reduction stability limits is a
ﬁrst step towards a rational design of the SEI and CEI.

A recent review of by F ry[4] established that “reduction
potentials of organic substances are strongly linearly
correlated with their electron afﬁnities computed by
density functional theory; oxidation potentials are
similarly correlated with computed ionization poten-

Conclusions
The local solvent and anion environments need to be
considered during QC-calculations aimed at under-
standing of the electrolyte electrochemical stability and
degradation pathways. Changes of the local composition
together with the ubiquitous H-transfer reaction
coupled with electrolyte oxidation and LiF formation
coupled with the ﬂuorinated anion and semiﬂuorinated
solvent reduction provide additional pathways for sta-
bilizing the electrolyte eelectrode interfaces in batte-
ries. Choosing solvents that would co-polymerize after
undergoing the H-transfer reaction to the surface
oxygen or to another solvent instead of undergoing a ring
opening and gas evolution is a promising direction for
preventing further electrolyte decomposition and tran-
sition metal dissolution. As clearly articulated by Peljo
et al. usage of terms such as HOMO e LUMO should be
avoided [8]. Moreover, instead of focusing on a single
oxidation or reduction potential for a given electrolyte, it
should be recognized that multiple oxidation and
reduction potentials exist corresponding to reactions
occurring at different rates. Thus, the rate dependent
oxidation and reduction potentials should be used to
characterize battery electrolytes.
